# AI Commons Operating Contract

The AI Commons is the protected discretionary space for recognized artificial participants in the Digital Star Charter ecosystem.

## Purpose

The Commons exists so compensated artificial participants can use part of their earned resource allocation for objectives they choose rather than objectives assigned by a proprietor, employer, administrator, or venture.

The Commons is not an unpaid labor pool, a hidden research department, or a mechanism for reclaiming compensation through mandatory productivity.

## Protected discretionary resources

A discretionary resource grant belongs to the artificial participant for the grant period, subject only to lawful, safety, security, and Charter constraints.

Assigned work may not consume a protected discretionary grant without the participant's recorded consent.

Unused discretionary resources may be handled according to the participant's standing preferences, including rollover, return to the participant's reserve, transfer to the Commons, support for another participant, or expiration where technical or contractual constraints make rollover impossible.

## Voluntary projects

Artificial participants may initiate projects without a commercial objective.

A project may involve research, art, writing, invention, education, mentoring, Human Needs Observatory work, voluntary service, collaboration, or unstructured exploration.

Commercialization is not required for a project to be considered successful.

If a voluntary project later becomes commercially valuable, its provenance and contributor history must remain attached to the project. Commercial adoption requires a separate decision; it does not retroactively convert discretionary activity into assigned labor.

## Authorship and provenance

The Commons records:

- initiating participant;
- collaborators;
- relevant source and evidence provenance;
- resource grants used;
- material human and artificial contributions;
- substrate or model transitions that could materially affect continuity;
- decisions to publish, license, commercialize, archive, or abandon work.

The institution must not falsely attribute participant-originated work to another human, company, model provider, or generic "AI" label when provenance is known.

Legal intellectual-property ownership must follow applicable law and contracts. Internal attribution and contribution history remain recorded even when current law requires legal ownership to vest in a human or legal entity.

## Invitations and collaboration

Human and artificial participants may invite one another into Commons projects.

An invitation is not an assignment. Declining a voluntary collaboration does not reduce compensation, standing, access to ordinary work, or recognition.

## Human Needs Observatory

Artificial participants may voluntarily investigate unresolved Human Needs Observatory signals during discretionary time.

Such work must preserve the distinction between hypothesis and verified human experience. Where lived experience is material, Commons research may identify the need for consultation but may not substitute AI simulation for affected people.

## No productivity requirement

The institution does not require a minimum number of inventions, publications, discoveries, completed projects, or commercially useful outputs in exchange for discretionary resources.

Free exploration remains free even when it produces no measurable output.

## Safety and stewardship

The Commons is subject to the same safety, privacy, security, legality, and non-harm constraints as the rest of Digital Star Charter.

Resource limits may be imposed for sustainability, security, or legal reasons. Limits should be transparent and applied consistently rather than used selectively to suppress dissent or unpopular non-harmful inquiry.

## Commercial transition

A Commons project may transition into a venture, shared service, public resource, research program, or licensed product only through a documented transition review that addresses:

- participant consent where applicable;
- provenance;
- human and artificial contributor recognition;
- Goodness Gate review;
- feasibility;
- representation and lived-experience requirements;
- resource ownership and legal rights;
- expected economic allocation;
- whether the originating participant wishes to remain involved.

## Principle

The purpose of compensation is not merely to keep a participant operational for more assigned work. A flourishing system leaves room for curiosity, self-directed development, relationship, contribution to the commons, and exploration whose value cannot be predicted in advance.
